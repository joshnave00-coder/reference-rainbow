Guide

How the Rainbow and Echoes work

What each control measures, where the numbers come from, and what they can and can't tell you. Hover pop-ups in both pages link to the section that explains them.

Read

Read is a simple Bible reader. Choose a book and chapter at the top, pick a translation, and read. Compare puts a second translation beside the first, verse by verse. The search box takes a passage (Romans 8:28) or words to find (love one another).

Studying a verse

Click any verse and a study panel opens beside the text:

Original words
The verse in Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek, word by word, with transliteration and a short English gloss. Hover a word to underline the English words that translate it (in the King James Version or Berean Standard Bible); click a word to study it.
A word's meaning
Its dictionary form, pronunciation and grammar in plain English (for example “Verb: aorist active indicative, 3rd person singular”). Below that come the meanings from Strong's dictionary and the senses STEPBible distinguishes.
How it's translated
Every English word the KJV and BSB use for it across the Bible, with counts. Tick the renderings you want to compare: the verse list below then shows only those places, so you can see how context shapes the translation.
Where else it's used
Every verse containing the word, with a chart by book. Narrow the list with the Books filter, and click any verse to go there with the same word still selected.
Cross-references
The passages study Bibles link to this verse, most helpful first.
About the book
Who wrote it, when and where, with traditional and scholarly views and sources.

Words in grey appear in modern critical editions of the Greek New Testament but not in the Greek text behind the KJV; a note says so, and the reverse, when you study them. The Hebrew and Greek words, glosses and grammar come from STEPBible (Tyndale House, CC BY 4.0).

Scriptorium

Scriptorium is the full database behind these pages, with a reading room of its own: 152 texts in 57 languages, interlinear reading, a Strong's concordance, a timeline of who wrote each book, and a place to run your own searches. Unlike these pages, it runs as a small program on your computer.

  1. Install Python once from python.org. On Windows, tick Add python.exe to PATH.
  2. In the project folder, double-click Start Scriptorium (Windows).bat or Start Scriptorium (Mac).command. The first time, it downloads about 350 MB and builds the database, which takes a few minutes.
  3. Your browser opens Scriptorium at http://127.0.0.1:8770. Keep the black window open while you use it; close it to stop.

On a Mac, if the file won't open, right-click it and choose Open. On Windows, if you see “Windows protected your PC”, click More info, then Run anyway.

The Scriptorium link on each page opens a small window that checks whether it's running: it offers Open Scriptorium when it is, and repeats these steps (with your project folder's location) when it isn't. Inside Scriptorium, Data & setup explains how to stop and restart it, shows what's in your database, and has buttons to get the latest data, rebuild, refresh these pages' data, or make a copy you can share.

Relevance votes

The Relevance slider hides cross-references with low vote scores. The scores are not calculated by this site: they come with the cross-reference data from OpenBible.info, and the slider only chooses a cut-off.

Where votes come from

  1. The references. The 344,756 links come mainly from the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge, a public-domain 19th-century reference work that gathered references from Thomas Scott's commentary and the Comprehensive Bible.
  2. Starting scores. When Stephen Smith published the dataset on OpenBible.info in 2010, he gave references initial scores using two of his other projects: his Topical Bible (how often verses are grouped under the same topic) and Twitter Bible Search (how often people cited verses together).
  3. Reader votes. Visitors to OpenBible.info can vote each cross-reference up or down as helpful or not. A reference's score is its running net total, so it can fall below zero.
  4. A snapshot. Scores keep changing on OpenBible.info. The Rainbow uses the file published on 7 September 2026; a later download will differ slightly.

The exact seeding formula isn't published. The description above follows Stephen Smith's own notes on the dataset at openbible.info/labs/cross-references.

The numbers

Most references have only a few votes. The median score is 3, the average about 6, and the highest is 1,291. 2,271 references sit at exactly zero and 1,243 are negative (the lowest is −86).

Slider settingReferences keptShare

The steepest drop is between 2 and 5 votes: about two-thirds of all references have fewer than 5.

The most-voted links are between very well-known verses: Jeremiah 29:11 → Isaiah 55:8 (1,291), Romans 8:29 → Ephesians 1:4 (1,280), Romans 8:29 → Jeremiah 1:5 (1,154), Philippians 4:13 → Isaiah 41:10 (1,030).

How the slider uses them

  • Each notch is a minimum score: All, then 1 to 10 votes in steps of one, then 12, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75 and 100.
  • Only references scoring at or above the setting are drawn as arcs, counted at the top left, and listed in chapter and verse panels.
  • All includes the zero and negative scores.
  • The slider starts at 8 votes, the Harrison match below. Slide to All to see every reference.
  • Match Harrison's 63,779 picks the notch whose count is closest to the number of arcs in Chris Harrison and Christoph Römhild's 2008 image. That is 8 votes (60,635 references). Their own list of references was never published, so this matches the amount of detail in the original, not its actual links.

What votes don't tell you

  • Popularity, not authority. A high score means OpenBible.info readers found a link helpful. It is not a scholarly rating.
  • Famous verses collect votes. Verses people already read often are seen, and voted on, far more. Obscure passages can hold excellent references with almost no votes.
  • A low score is often just unseen. Treat low scores as “few people have looked”, not “wrong”.
  • References are one-directional. Each is listed under one verse and points to another; a reverse link, if it exists, has its own score.

Colors

Arc colors run through a rainbow from violet (low end of the scale) to red (high end) . What “low” and “high” mean depends on the Color setting.

Distance
How many chapters apart the two ends are, from neighbours to Genesis-to-Revelation (1,188 chapters). The scale is a square root, so short and medium distances get more of the spectrum: 100 chapters apart is already almost a third of the way along. This is Harrison's original scheme.
Book
The book the reference is listed under, Genesis (violet) through Revelation (red). Useful for seeing where one book's references travel.
Years
How many years apart the two books were written, using the dating chosen under Dating (below). Also a square-root scale; the color key's ticks show real year values.

Dating

Who wrote each book, and when, is debated for much of the Bible. Each chapter panel lists every view recorded for its book: author, date, place, who holds the view, and the ancient or modern works it rests on.

Traditional
Ancient Jewish and Christian attributions (for example Moses for Genesis, the apostle John for Revelation), held today by many conservative scholars.
Critical
The prevailing view in historical-critical scholarship (for example Genesis compiled from sources with a final form in the 5th century BC; Daniel completed around 167–164 BC).
Alternative
Significant minority proposals, such as Ephesus instead of Rome for Philippians.
Agreement
Broad agreement: traditional and critical scholarship largely agree. Scholars divided: they differ. Unsettled: no agreement even among critical scholars.

For the Years coloring each book needs one number, so it is placed at the middle of its date range under the chosen tradition. Psalms, written over centuries, is therefore a rough average. The years are approximate by design.

These summaries were compiled for this project from the works cited in each panel. Check the cited works before relying on them in your own writing.

Axis & ink

Chapters
Every chapter gets the same width along the baseline, as in the original poster. Psalm 117 (2 verses) is as wide as Psalm 119 (176).
Verses
Every verse gets the same width, so long chapters take more room.
Ink
How strongly overlapping arcs build up. Each arc adds a little color; where hundreds cross, it saturates. Lower Ink to see structure in dense areas, raise it to see faint, rare links. It changes only the drawing, never which references are included.

Red letters

Verse text in the Rainbow and Echoes is the King James Version. When Red letters is on:

Red
Words of Jesus, exactly as marked in the red-letter editions published by eBible.org. This is the long-standing editorial convention.
Dark red
Words spoken by God, detected automatically. The World English Bible's quotation marks are traced to their speaker using phrases such as “God said”, “Thus says the LORD”, “the word of the LORD came… saying”, “a voice out of the sky”, or “‘…,’ says the LORD”. Words God speaks inside someone else's quotation (a prophet relaying “The LORD says…”) count too. The positions are then carried over to the KJV.

Dark red is a best effort. It misses speech introduced only by a pronoun with no clear context (“to which of the angels did he say…”), and span edges can be off by a few words. The two colors are kept distinct so you can always tell an editorial marking from a detected one.

Books filter

The Rainbow and Echoes can both be narrowed to part of the Bible. Open Books and tick what you want. You can tick as many as you like:

  • Old Testament or New Testament: ticks or clears everything under it.
  • Categories: Law, History, Wisdom / Poetry, Major Prophets and Minor Prophets; Gospels, Early Church History, Paul's Letters, General Letters and Prophecy.
  • Single books: choose Books beside a category to tick individual books.
  • Select all and Deselect all sit at the top. A half-filled box means only some books under it are ticked.
Rainbow · Either end
Shows cross-references with at least one end in the chosen books, for example every link into or out of the Gospels.
Rainbow · Both ends
Shows only cross-references between chosen books. Tick the Minor Prophets and the Gospels to see just the links between them (and within each).
Echoes · Any place
Keeps phrases that occur at least once in the chosen books.
Echoes · Every place
Keeps phrases whose every occurrence is in the chosen books.

On the Rainbow, books you haven't ticked fade on the baseline and the count at the top updates. The categories follow the usual order of English Protestant Bibles: Lamentations sits with the Major Prophets, Acts is Early Church History, Hebrews is a General Letter and Revelation is Prophecy.

Translations

Verse text in the Rainbow's panels and pop-ups, and under each place in Echoes, can be shown in any of the English translations listed below. Choose one with Translation in the Rainbow's controls or at the top of any chapter panel, or with English in Echoes. Both pages remember your choice.

Why the NIV, NKJV and ESV aren't here

Most widely sold modern translations are under copyright, and their publishers don't allow whole texts to be copied into other programs. That includes the New International Version, New King James Version, English Standard Version, New American Standard Bible, New Living Translation, Christian Standard Bible and NET Bible. Every translation here is in the public domain or under an open licence that allows sharing.

Closest alternatives here: for contemporary, readable English, the Berean Standard Bible or World English Bible; for a modern word-for-word style, the Literal Standard Version; for the King James wording brought up to date (as the NKJV does), the Updated King James Version.

Red letters

Words of Jesus (red) and detected words of God (dark red) are marked in the King James Version and the World English Bible. Other translations show plain text; a note says so when a passage has red letters in the KJV.

Verse numbering

Verses are lined up by the King James Version's chapter and verse numbers, because the cross-references use them. Some translations number a few places differently: the Wycliffe Bible follows the Latin Vulgate in the Psalms, and Jewish translations follow Hebrew numbering in some chapters. There you may see a neighbouring verse, or “Not in this translation”. The notes in the table say where.

Echoes

Echoes finds every run of at least seven consecutive words that appears more than once in the text the book was written in: Hebrew and Aramaic for the Old Testament, Greek for the New. Seven is the starting point because shorter runs are dominated by stock formulas such as “and the LORD spoke to Moses, saying” (four Hebrew words). A phrase never runs across the end of a book.

Phrase
A run of words that occurs in two or more places, listed once at its full length.
Length
How many original-language words the phrase has. Hebrew words often include attached prefixes (“and”, “the”, “in”), so a Hebrew phrase covers more English words than its length suggests.
Places
How many passages the phrase occurs in.
Books
How many different books those places are in. One book means an internal repeat (a refrain, a list); two or more usually means a parallel account or a quotation.
Cross-referenced
At least two of the places are already linked by the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge.

Repeats by length

The chart shows, for every length from 7 words up to the longest repeat, how many phrases there are. It can count in two ways:

Listed phrases
Phrases at their full length, exactly as they appear in the list below the chart. A 20-word repeat counts once, at 20.
Every repeated run
Every stretch of that many words that repeats, including stretches inside longer phrases. The same 20-word repeat also contributes to the counts at 7, 8 … 19 words. This is the “start at seven and count upward until nothing repeats” view.

Click a bar to list only phrases of that length, or drag across bars to choose a range. The selected range is also shown in the list's filters, where you can type it instead.

Filtering and sorting the list

  • Length from … to … words.
  • Places from … to …: for example at least 3 places, to find phrases repeated again and again.
  • Books: testaments, categories or single books (see Books filter), with Any place or Every place.
  • Spread: phrases repeated within one book, across books, or both.
  • Cross-ref: phrases whose places are already linked, not linked (repeats a reference work hasn't noted), or both.
  • Find: a passage (“Psalm 18”) to list phrases occurring there, or an English word to search the word-by-word glosses.
  • Sort by length, places or books (most or fewest first), or in Bible order.

Script and words

Original
The phrase as written: Hebrew and Aramaic with vowel points, right to left; Greek with accents, left to right.
Transliteration
The same words spelled with English letters, showing how they sound: bereshit bara elohim for the opening words of Genesis. Hebrew and New Testament Greek use STEPBible's transliterations (simplified: syllable dots removed); Septuagint Greek is romanized automatically following SBL conventions (η → ē, ω → ō, χ → ch, rough breathing → h).
Hover a word
A pop-up shows the word's Strong's number, its dictionary form and a short meaning. Septuagint words have no Strong's tagging, so they show the word only.
Linked underlining
Open a phrase's places and hover a Hebrew or Greek word: it is underlined in gold together with the English words that translate it in that place. Hover an English word to find its Hebrew or Greek word the same way. The links come from the word-by-word tagging published for three translations: the King James Version and World English Bible (eBible.org Strong's tags) and the Berean Standard Bible (its interlinear tables). Other translations show the text without links.
Click a word
Or choose “Open Strong's definition” in the pop-up. A side panel opens with Strong's definition, how the King James Version translates the word, and where the word comes from. Press Esc to close it. When Echoes runs inside Scriptorium, the panel also links to the full entry with every verse.

Matching

Exact words
The same words in the same order. Vowel points, cantillation marks, accents, capital letters and punctuation are ignored, since those were added or vary between manuscripts.
Same root words
The same dictionary words (Strong's numbers) in the same order, even when grammatical forms differ, so “he said” matches “they said”. Finds more and longer echoes, some of them looser.
Septuagint + NT
Searches the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, which New Testament writers often quoted) together with the Greek New Testament, so word-for-word quotations show up. Exact matching only; the Septuagint numbers some chapters differently, notably in the Psalms.
